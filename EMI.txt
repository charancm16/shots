# 2-Mark Q&A: Electronic Measurements & Instrumentation

*Each unit has 15 questions. Each answer is short enough for a 2-mark exam response, with a simple example.*

## UNIT I: Measuring Instruments & Digital Instruments

**Q1. What is accuracy?**
Accuracy is how close a measured value is to the true value.
*Example: A meter reading 9.9 V when the true voltage is 10 V has high accuracy.*

**Q2. What is precision?**
Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, regardless of the true value.
*Example: Readings of 9.8 V, 9.8 V, and 9.8 V are precise, even if the true value is 10 V.*

**Q3. What is resolution?**
Resolution is the smallest change in the input that an instrument can detect and display.
*Example: A digital meter that shows 0.01 V steps has a resolution of 0.01 V.*

**Q4. What are significant figures?**
Significant figures are the digits in a measured value that carry meaningful information, including the last uncertain digit.
*Example: The value 2.50 has three significant figures.*

**Q5. What is an error in measurement?**
Error is the difference between the measured value and the true value.
*Example: If the true voltage is 100 V and the meter reads 98 V, the error is -2 V.*

**Q6. What is the construction of a PMMC meter?**
A PMMC (Permanent Magnet Moving Coil) meter has a coil mounted on a pivot inside the field of a permanent magnet, with a pointer and a spring.
*Example: The basic D'Arsonval galvanometer is a PMMC meter.*

**Q7. What is the working principle of a PMMC meter?**
Current through the coil produces a torque that deflects the pointer, and the spring provides a restoring torque. The deflection is proportional to the current.
*Example: If the current doubles, the pointer deflection roughly doubles.*

**Q8. What is range extension?**
Range extension increases an instrument's measuring range by adding a shunt resistor for current or a multiplier resistor for voltage.
*Example: A 1 mA meter with a suitable shunt can be used to measure 10 A.*

**Q9. What is a multirange voltmeter?**
A multirange voltmeter uses switchable series resistors to give several voltage ranges on one meter.
*Example: One meter with 0-10 V, 0-50 V, and 0-250 V ranges.*

**Q10. What is a multimeter?**
A multimeter is an instrument that measures voltage, current, and resistance.
*Example: Use it to check a battery's voltage and test whether a fuse is continuous.*

**Q11. What is a digital voltmeter (DVM)?**
A DVM measures voltage and shows the result as a numerical display.
*Example: A DVM displaying 5.02 V.*

**Q12. What is a V-T type DVM?**
A V-T DVM converts the input voltage into a time interval, and a counter measures that interval to give a digital reading.
*Example: A 2 V input produces a longer ramp time than a 1 V input, and the counter shows the difference.*

**Q13. What is a V-F type DVM?**
A V-F DVM converts the input voltage into a frequency, and a counter counts the pulses over a fixed time.
*Example: A 1 V input may produce 1000 pulses per second, which the counter displays as 1000 counts.*

**Q14. What is a successive approximation DVM?**
It finds the digital value by testing bits one at a time, from the most significant to the least significant, using a DAC and a comparator.
*Example: An 8-bit successive approximation converter needs only 8 comparisons.*

**Q15. What is a digital frequency meter?**
A digital frequency meter counts the cycles of a signal within a fixed gate time and displays the frequency.
*Example: Counting 5000 cycles in 1 second gives a reading of 5 kHz.*

## UNIT II: Oscilloscopes

**Q1. What is a CRO?**
A CRO (Cathode Ray Oscilloscope) displays the waveform of an electrical signal on a screen.
*Example: Viewing the sine wave output of an audio amplifier.*

**Q2. What are the main blocks of a CRO?**
The main blocks are the CRT, vertical amplifier, horizontal (time base) system, trigger circuit, and power supply.
*Example: The vertical amplifier boosts the input signal before it reaches the CRT's deflection plates.*

**Q3. What is the basic principle of a CRT?**
An electron beam is emitted, accelerated, focused, and deflected onto a phosphor-coated screen, where it produces a glowing spot.
*Example: The spot traces a waveform as the beam moves across the screen.*

**Q4. What is the construction of a CRT?**
A CRT has an electron gun, deflection plates, and a phosphor screen, all sealed inside a glass envelope.
*Example: The inner face of the screen is coated with phosphor that glows when struck by electrons.*

**Q5. What is the role of the vertical amplifier?**
It amplifies the input signal so that it deflects the beam vertically by a visible amount.
*Example: A 10 mV signal is amplified so the trace is clearly visible on the screen.*

**Q6. What is the horizontal deflection system?**
It moves the electron beam horizontally across the screen at a controlled rate, creating a time axis.
*Example: A setting of 1 ms/div makes each screen division represent 1 millisecond.*

**Q7. What is sweep?**
Sweep is a linear ramp voltage that moves the beam across the screen at a constant speed.
*Example: A sawtooth voltage is commonly used to produce the sweep.*

**Q8. What is a trigger pulse?**
A trigger pulse starts the sweep at the same point on each input cycle, which keeps the waveform stable on the screen.
*Example: The sweep starts at the zero-crossing of the signal.*

**Q9. What is a delay line?**
A delay line delays the signal reaching the vertical plates so that the leading edge of the signal is visible.
*Example: It lets you see the start of a fast pulse that would otherwise be missed.*

**Q10. What is a sync selector circuit?**
A sync selector chooses the source of the triggering signal, such as internal, line, or external.
*Example: Selecting "internal" triggers the sweep from the signal being observed.*

**Q11. What is a dual beam CRO?**
A dual beam CRO uses two electron beams in one CRT to display two signals at the same time.
*Example: Comparing the input and output of an amplifier on the same screen.*

**Q12. What is a dual trace CRO?**
A dual trace CRO uses one beam that is switched electronically between two signals to show two traces.
*Example: Displaying two signals in alternate or chopped mode.*

**Q13. What is a sampling oscilloscope?**
A sampling oscilloscope takes samples of a repetitive signal at slightly delayed points and builds up the waveform from them.
*Example: Used to view very high-frequency signals in the GHz range.*

**Q14. What is a digital storage oscilloscope?**
A digital storage oscilloscope stores the waveform in digital memory so it can be held, viewed, and analysed later.
*Example: Capturing a single glitch that would be hard to see on an ordinary CRO.*

**Q15. What is a Lissajous figure?**
A Lissajous figure is the pattern produced when sine signals are applied to the X and Y inputs of a CRO, and it is used to compare frequencies.
*Example: A circle appears when the two frequencies are equal and the phase difference is 90°.*

## UNIT III: Bridges

**Q1. What is a bridge circuit?**
A bridge circuit is a null-type circuit that balances two arms to find an unknown component, with zero reading on the detector at balance.
*Example: The Wheatstone bridge balances to find an unknown resistance.*

**Q2. What is a Wheatstone bridge used for?**
It is used to measure medium-range resistance accurately.
*Example: Measuring the value of a 100 Ω resistor.*

**Q3. What is the balance condition of a Wheatstone bridge?**
At balance, R1/R2 = R3/R4, so the unknown resistance is Rx = R2 × R3 / R1.
*Example: If R1 = R2 = 100 Ω and R3 = 50 Ω, then Rx = 50 Ω.*

**Q4. What is a Kelvin bridge used for?**
A Kelvin bridge measures very low resistances (below about 1 Ω) accurately by eliminating lead and contact resistance.
*Example: Measuring the resistance of a current shunt.*

**Q5. What is a Maxwell's bridge used for?**
Maxwell's bridge measures an unknown inductance by comparing it with a known capacitance.
*Example: Measuring the inductance of a coil with a low Q factor.*

**Q6. What is a Hay's bridge used for?**
Hay's bridge measures inductance of coils with a high Q factor (above about 10).
*Example: Measuring a high-quality RF coil.*

**Q7. What is an Anderson bridge used for?**
Anderson's bridge measures self-inductance accurately by comparing it with a standard capacitor and resistors.
*Example: Finding the self-inductance of a laboratory standard coil.*

**Q8. What is a Schering bridge used for?**
A Schering bridge measures capacitance and the dielectric loss of insulating materials.
*Example: Testing the insulation of a power cable.*

**Q9. What is a Wien bridge used for?**
A Wien bridge measures capacitance and frequency, and it is also used in oscillator circuits.
*Example: The Wien bridge oscillator produces sine waves in the audio range.*

**Q10. What are the source and detector in a bridge?**
A DC bridge uses a DC source, and an AC bridge uses an AC source. The detector is usually a galvanometer for DC, and headphones or a CRO for AC.
*Example: An AC bridge with headphones as detector gives a sound minimum at balance.*

**Q11. What are the common errors in bridge measurements?**
Common errors include lead resistance, stray capacitance, temperature changes, and detector sensitivity.
*Example: Long test leads add extra resistance to a Wheatstone bridge reading.*

**Q12. What precautions should be taken while using bridges?**
Use the correct source and detector, keep the source switched off when not balancing, use short leads, and shield the circuit from stray fields.
*Example: Switching off the battery prevents the bridge resistors from heating and changing value.*

**Q13. Why is balancing an AC bridge more difficult than a DC bridge?**
An AC bridge must balance both magnitude and phase, so two adjustments are usually needed.
*Example: Balancing a Maxwell's bridge requires adjusting both a resistor and a variable element.*

**Q14. Why is the Kelvin bridge preferred for low resistance?**
It uses a four-terminal connection that separates current and voltage leads, removing the effect of lead resistance.
*Example: A low-value resistor is measured accurately with the four terminals.*

**Q15. What is the main advantage of bridge methods?**
Bridges give high accuracy because they measure at a null point, so the result does not depend on the exact source voltage.
*Example: A small change in battery voltage does not change the balance point.*

## UNIT IV: Signal Generators

**Q1. What is a signal generator?**
A signal generator is an instrument that produces test signals of known frequency, amplitude, and waveform.
*Example: Feeding a sine wave into an amplifier to test its response.*

**Q2. What is a fixed AF oscillator?**
A fixed AF oscillator produces one fixed audio frequency.
*Example: A 1 kHz test tone used for calibration.*

**Q3. What is a variable AF oscillator?**
A variable AF oscillator produces audio frequencies that can be adjusted over a range.
*Example: An RC oscillator with a variable resistor to change its frequency.*

**Q4. What is a standard signal generator?**
A standard signal generator produces an RF signal with accurately known frequency and output level, controlled by an attenuator.
*Example: Testing the sensitivity of a radio receiver.*

**Q5. What is a laboratory type signal generator?**
It is a general-purpose generator that provides sine, square, and triangle outputs over a wide frequency range.
*Example: Used in college labs for many experiments.*

**Q6. What is an AF sine wave generator?**
It produces a sine wave in the audio range, usually using an RC or Wien bridge oscillator.
*Example: A 20 Hz to 20 kHz sine wave used to test audio equipment.*

**Q7. What is a square wave generator?**
It produces a square wave, often using a multivibrator or a Schmitt trigger.
*Example: Generating a clock signal to test digital circuits.*

**Q8. What is a function generator?**
A function generator produces sine, square, and triangle waveforms from a single unit.
*Example: Testing a filter's response with different waveforms.*

**Q9. What is a pulse generator?**
A pulse generator produces pulses with adjustable width, period, and rise time.
*Example: Creating a 10 µs pulse to test a digital counter.*

**Q10. What is a sweep frequency generator?**
A sweep frequency generator changes its output frequency linearly over a range. It is used with a CRO to view frequency response.
*Example: Plotting the frequency response of an amplifier on the screen.*

**Q11. What is the difference between a square wave and a pulse generator?**
A square wave has a 50% duty cycle, while a pulse generator allows the duty cycle to be adjusted.
*Example: A pulse with a 10% duty cycle is not a square wave.*

**Q12. What is the function of an attenuator in a signal generator?**
An attenuator reduces the output amplitude in calibrated steps, usually in decibels.
*Example: A -20 dB step reduces the output to one-tenth of its amplitude.*

**Q13. Why is waveform purity important in a signal generator?**
Distortion or harmonics in the output can give wrong test results.
*Example: A sine wave with strong harmonics can make an amplifier look less linear than it is.*

**Q14. What is a triangle wave generator?**
A triangle wave generator produces a signal that rises and falls linearly.
*Example: A triangle wave is often used as a sweep signal.*

**Q15. What is the use of a signal generator in the laboratory?**
It provides a known input to a circuit so that the circuit can be tested, measured, and calibrated.
*Example: Injecting a known signal into a radio IF stage to check its gain.*
